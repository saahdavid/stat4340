Point Estimation

Estimate a parameter with one number: bias, standard error, the method of moments, and maximum likelihood.

1. Point estimators and point estimates

Let θ be an unknown population parameter. A point estimate is a single number that serves as a reasonable guess for θ. It comes from choosing a suitable statistic and computing it from the sample. That statistic is the point estimator, written \hat\theta. Because \hat\theta is computed from a random sample it has sampling variability: \hat\theta=\theta+\text{estimation error}.

  • Identify the parameter, the estimator, and the estimate in a study.
  • Decide whether an estimator is unbiased and find its bias.
  • Compare unbiased estimators by their variances; recognize the MVUE.
  • Compute the standard error of x̄ and of p̂.
  • Find method of moments and maximum likelihood estimators, and use the invariance property.
Worked example

Chevy Spark fuel economy

A study wants the center of the MPG distribution of the new Chevy Spark and records the MPG of a random sample of 100 cars.

  1. Parameter: θ = μ, the mean MPG of all new Chevy Sparks.
  2. Estimator: \hat\theta=\bar X, the sample mean MPG of 100 randomly chosen cars (a random variable).
  3. Estimate: the observed value \bar x from this particular sample, for example 31.2 MPG.
Watch out. The estimator is the rule (a random variable, like \bar X); the estimate is the number it produces for one sample (like \bar x=31.2).

2. Several estimators for the same parameter

Different statistics can estimate the same parameter. For a symmetric population, the mean μ is also the median, so several sensible estimators exist.

Worked example

Dielectric breakdown voltage (Devore, Example 6.2)

Twenty observations on breakdown voltage for pieces of epoxy resin are assumed to come from a normal distribution with mean μ:

24.46, 25.61, 26.25, 26.42, 26.66, 27.15, 27.31, 27.54, 27.74, 27.94, 27.98, 28.04, 28.28, 28.49, 28.50, 28.87, 29.11, 29.13, 29.50, 30.88

  1. (a) Sample mean: \bar x=555.86/20=27.793.
  2. (b) Sample median: \tilde x=(27.94+27.98)/2=27.960.
  3. (c) Average of the two extremes: (24.46+30.88)/2=27.670.
  4. (d) 10% trimmed mean (drop the smallest and largest 2 values, then average): (555.86-24.46-25.61-29.50-30.88)/16=27.838.
  5. Which is “best”? All four aim at μ; the next sections give the criteria: unbiasedness and small variance.

3. Unbiased estimators

An estimator is unbiased when its sampling distribution is centered at the parameter, for every possible value of θ. Otherwise it is biased.

\text{Unbiased: }E(\hat\theta)=\theta \text{Bias}(\hat\theta)=E(\hat\theta)-\theta

Picture taking many random samples of 100 Chevy Sparks and computing \bar x each time. If the population mean is 30 MPG, a histogram of all those sample means is centered very close to 30: \bar X is unbiased.

  • If X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p), the sample proportion \hat p=X/n is unbiased for p.
  • For a random sample with mean μ and finite variance σ², the sample mean \hat\mu=\bar X is unbiased for μ, and the sample variance S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2 is unbiased for σ².
  • If the population is continuous and symmetric about μ, the sample median is also unbiased for μ.
Worked example

Blue whales

Decibel levels are recorded for 50 blue whales; X = number above 180 dB, X\sim\text{Bin}(50,p). In one sample X = 33.

  1. Point estimate: \hat p=33/50=0.66.
  2. Repeating the survey many times gives estimates \hat p_1,\hat p_2,\hat p_3,\ldots; their histogram is centered at the true p, because E(\hat p)=E(X)/n=np/n=p.
Worked example

Dividing by n is biased

  1. Let \hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\frac{n-1}{n}S^2.
  2. E(\hat\sigma^2)=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2, so \text{Bias}=-\frac{\sigma^2}{n}: it underestimates σ² on average.
  3. The bias shrinks to 0 as n grows. This is one reason the sample variance divides by n − 1.

4. Minimum variance unbiased estimators

Two desirable properties for an estimator are unbiasedness, E(\hat\theta)=\theta, and minimum variance: among all unbiased estimators of θ, it has the smallest variance, so it varies least from sample to sample. An estimator with both properties is the minimum variance unbiased estimator (MVUE).

\text{Normal sample: }\hat\mu=\bar X \text{is the MVUE of }\mu
Worked example

Two unbiased estimators of μ

From a random sample X_1,\ldots,X_n with variance σ², compare \hat\mu_1=\bar X and \hat\mu_2=\frac{X_1+X_n}{2}.

  1. Both are unbiased: E(\hat\mu_2)=\frac{\mu+\mu}{2}=\mu.
  2. \text{Var}(\hat\mu_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n} and \text{Var}(\hat\mu_2)=\frac{\sigma^2+\sigma^2}{4}=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}.
  3. For n > 2, \bar X has the smaller variance, so it is the better choice: it uses all the data.

5. The standard error

The standard error of an estimator is its standard deviation. It measures the precision of the estimator: how much it varies from sample to sample.

\sigma_{\hat\theta}=\sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat\theta)} \sigma_{\bar X}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}

When σ or p is unknown, replace it by its estimate to get the estimated standard error, such as s/\sqrt n or \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}.

Worked example

Standard errors

  1. Breakdown voltage: s = 1.462 with n = 20, so the estimated SE of \bar X is 1.462/\sqrt{20}=0.327.
  2. Blue whales: \hat p=0.66, n = 50, so the estimated SE is \sqrt{0.66(0.34)/50}=0.067.

6. The method of moments

For a random sample X_1,\ldots,X_n, the kth population moment is E(X^k) and the kth sample moment is \frac1n\sum X_i^k. The mean is the first moment, and \text{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2; if the mean is 0, the variance is just the second moment.

With m unknown parameters, the method of moments (MoM) sets the first m sample moments equal to the corresponding population moments and solves for the parameters.

\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^j=E(X^j),\quad j=1,\ldots,m
Worked example

Three MoM estimators

  1. Normal N(μ, σ²). E(X)=\mu and E(X^2)=\sigma^2+\mu^2. So \hat\mu=\bar X and \hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum X_i^2-\bar X^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2.
  2. Exponential(λ). E(X)=1/\lambda=\bar X, so \hat\lambda=1/\bar X.
  3. Gamma(α, β). E(X)=\alpha\beta and \text{Var}(X)=\alpha\beta^2. Matching \bar X and \hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2: \hat\beta=\hat\sigma^2/\bar X and \hat\alpha=\bar X^2/\hat\sigma^2.
Watch out. MoM matches moments with the divisor n: \frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2, not S^2.

7. Maximum likelihood estimation

For observed values x_1,\ldots,x_n from a PMF or PDF f(x;\theta), the likelihood function is the joint PMF or PDF of the data, viewed as a function of the parameters. The maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) are the parameter values that maximize it: the values that make the observed data most plausible under the model.

L(\theta)=f(x_1,\ldots,x_n;\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i;\theta) L(\hat\theta)\ge L(\theta)\text{ for all }\theta

Recipe. Write L, take \ell=\ln L (products become sums), set \ell'(\theta)=0, and solve.

Worked example

Exponential MLE

  1. L(\lambda)=\prod\lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}=\lambda^ne^{-\lambda\sum x_i}.
  2. \ell(\lambda)=n\ln\lambda-\lambda\sum x_i and \ell'(\lambda)=\frac n\lambda-\sum x_i=0.
  3. \hat\lambda=\frac{n}{\sum x_i}=\frac1{\bar x}: the same as the MoM estimator here.
Worked example

Normal MLEs

  1. \ell(\mu,\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i-\mu)^2.
  2. Setting the partial derivatives to 0 gives \hat\mu=\bar x and \hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2.
  3. The MLE of σ² divides by n, so it is slightly biased; for large n the difference from S^2 is tiny.

8. Invariance and large-sample behavior of the MLE

Invariance. If \hat\theta_1,\ldots,\hat\theta_m are MLEs, the MLE of any function h(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_m) is h(\hat\theta_1,\ldots,\hat\theta_m).

\widehat{h(\theta)}=h(\hat\theta) \hat\sigma=\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2}=\sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}
Worked example

Using invariance

  1. Normal sample: the MLE of σ is \sqrt{\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}.
  2. Exponential lifetimes with \bar x=5 hours: \hat\lambda=0.2, so the MLE of P(X > 8) = e^{-8\lambda} is e^{-8(0.2)}=0.2019.

Large-sample behavior. For large n the MLE is approximately unbiased (E(\hat\theta)\approx\theta) and approximately efficient: its variance is about as small as possible among unbiased estimators. So for large n, the MLE is approximately the MVUE.

9. Summary

An estimator is a statistic used to guess a parameter. Prefer estimators that are unbiased and have small variance, and report precision with the standard error. The method of moments matches sample and population moments; maximum likelihood maximizes L(θ), and its estimates transform by invariance.

\text{Bias}=E(\hat\theta)-\theta \sigma_{\bar X}=\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\tfrac{p(1-p)}{n}} \text{Exp: }\hat\lambda=1/\bar x
