Hypothesis Testing

Test a claim about μ or p with a test statistic, a rejection region or a p-value, and an honest conclusion.

1. Statistical hypotheses

A statistical hypothesis is a claim about an unknown population parameter. “The mean student debt of all US college students is greater than $43,500” is \mu>43{,}500; “the proportion who voted for Candidate A is less than 24%” is p<0.24. Hypothesis testing uses sample data to judge whether such a claim is plausible.

  • Write H₀ and Hₐ for a claim about μ or p.
  • Follow the steps of a test: parameter, hypotheses, test statistic, p-value or rejection region, decision, conclusion.
  • Run the z test for μ (σ known), the large-sample z test (n > 40), the t test (df = n − 1), and the z test for p.
  • Decide with the critical value (rejection region) or with the p-value; both always agree.
  • Explain Type I and Type II errors and the trade-off between α and β.

The null hypothesis H₀ is the initial claim, assumed true unless there is strong evidence against it; it always uses “=”. The alternative hypothesis Hₐ is the competing claim. The two possible conclusions are reject H₀ in favor of Hₐ, or fail to reject H₀.

H_0:\mu=\mu_0\ \text{vs}\ H_a:\mu<\mu_0 H_a:\mu>\mu_0 H_a:\mu\ne\mu_0

These are the lower-tailed, upper-tailed, and two-tailed tests. Tests for p use the same three forms.

Worked example

Kingfisher flight speed

Scientists claim kingfishers fly faster than 26 mph on average; a random sample of 30 birds is used.

  1. Parameter: μ, the mean flight speed of all kingfishers.
  2. The claim to find evidence for goes in Hₐ: H_0:\mu=26 versus H_a:\mu>26.
Watch out. Hypotheses are about parameters (μ, p), never statistics (x̄, p̂). And H₀ always contains the equality.

2. The steps of a hypothesis test

  1. Step 0. Identify the parameter and describe it in context, in words.
  2. Step 1. State H₀ and Hₐ.
  3. Step 2. Compute the test statistic.
  4. Step 3. Find the p-value (or the rejection region).
  5. Step 4. Decide: reject H₀ or fail to reject H₀.
  6. Step 5. State the conclusion in the context of the problem, in words.

A test statistic measures how far the sample data are from what H₀ predicts. The rejection region is the set of test-statistic values for which we reject H₀. Assuming H₀ is true, the p-value is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. A smaller p-value is stronger evidence against H₀.

\text{Reject }H_0\text{ if p-value}<\alpha \text{equivalently, if TS is in the rejection region}

The significance level α (between 0 and 1) is chosen before the test: it is the probability of rejecting H₀ when H₀ is true.

Two methods, one decision. With the critical-value method, find the critical value from the table and check whether TS falls in the rejection region. With the p-value method, find the tail area beyond TS and compare it with α. Use either one in practice and on exams; they always agree.

3. Type I and Type II errors

DecisionH₀ trueH₀ false
Reject H₀Type I error (probability α)Correct (power = 1 − β)
Fail to reject H₀CorrectType II error (probability β)
P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ true})=\alpha P(\text{fail to reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ false})=\beta

Proposition. With n and the test statistic fixed, decreasing α shrinks the rejection region and increases β. Choose α carefully: there is a trade-off between the two error probabilities.

Watch out. A Type I error can only happen when you reject H₀; a Type II error only when you fail to reject. And “fail to reject H₀” does not prove H₀ true.

4. The z test for μ (σ known)

Assumptions: a random sample from a normal population with known σ; any n.

Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)\text{ under }H_0
H_a:\mu>\mu_0:\ P(Z>z) H_a:\mu<\mu_0:\ P(Z<z) H_a:\mu\ne\mu_0:\ 2P(Z>|z|)

The rejection regions are z\ge z_\alpha, z\le -z_\alpha, and |z|\ge z_{\alpha/2}.

Worked example

Sprinkler activation temperature

A manufacturer claims the mean activation temperature is 130 °F. Nine systems average 131.08 °F; temperatures are normal with σ = 1.5 °F. Does this contradict the claim at α = 0.01?

  1. μ = mean activation temperature of all these systems. H_0:\mu=130 vs H_a:\mu\ne130.
  2. z=\frac{131.08-130}{1.5/\sqrt9}=\frac{1.08}{0.5}=2.16.
  3. Critical value: z_{0.005}=2.576; reject if |z| ≥ 2.576. |2.16| < 2.576.
  4. p-value: 2P(Z>2.16)=2(1-0.9846)=0.0308>0.01.
  5. Fail to reject H₀. At the 0.01 level the data do not contradict the claim that the mean activation temperature is 130 °F.
Rz <- (131.08 - 130)/(1.5/3); 2 * (1 - pnorm(abs(z)))
Pythonfrom scipy.stats import norm; z = (131.08 - 130)/(1.5/3); 2 * norm.sf(abs(z))

5. The large-sample z test for μ

Assumptions: any population shape, σ unknown, and a large sample: n > 40. Replace σ with s; the p-values and rejection regions are the same as in Section 4.

Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{S/\sqrt n}\approx N(0,1)
Worked example

Zinc intake

The recommended daily zinc allowance for men over 50 is 15 mg/day. For a sample of men aged 65–74, n = 115, \bar x=11.3, s = 6.43. Does mean intake fall below 15? Use α = 0.05.

  1. μ = mean daily zinc intake of all men aged 65–74. H_0:\mu=15 vs H_a:\mu<15.
  2. n = 115 > 40: large-sample z test. SE = 6.43/√115 = 0.5996.
  3. z=(11.3-15)/0.5996=-6.17.
  4. Critical value -z_{0.05}=-1.645: −6.17 ≤ −1.645. p-value = P(Z < −6.17), essentially 0 (beyond the table).
  5. Reject H₀. There is strong evidence that the mean daily zinc intake of men aged 65–74 is below the recommended 15 mg/day.

6. The t test for μ

Assumptions: a random sample from a normal population with σ unknown; n need not be large. Course rule: use df = n − 1 exactly and read the t table in that row.

T=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}\text{ under }H_0
H_a:\mu>\mu_0:\ P(T>t) H_a:\mu<\mu_0:\ P(T<t) H_a:\mu\ne\mu_0:\ 2P(T>|t|)

The t table brackets a p-value between two column areas (double them for ≠); software gives the exact value.

Worked example

Time perception during nicotine withdrawal

After 24 hours without smoking, 20 smokers estimated a 45-second period: 69, 56, 65, 50, 72, 70, 73, 47, 59, 56, 55, 45, 39, 70, 52, 64, 67, 67, 57, 53. Does the true mean perceived time differ from 45 seconds? Use α = 0.05 and assume normality.

  1. μ = mean perceived time for all smokers after 24 hours of abstinence. H_0:\mu=45 vs H_a:\mu\ne45.
  2. \bar x=1186/20=59.3, s = 9.836, SE = 9.836/√20 = 2.199.
  3. t=(59.3-45)/2.199=6.50, df = 19.
  4. Critical value: t_{0.025,19}=2.093; |6.50| ≥ 2.093. p-value: 6.50 is beyond the 0.0005 column (3.883), so p-value < 2(0.0005) = 0.001. Software: 0.000003.
  5. Reject H₀. There is strong evidence that the mean perceived time differs from 45 seconds (it is longer).
Rt.test(c(69, 56, 65, 50, 72, 70, 73, 47, 59, 56, 55, 45, 39, 70, 52, 64, 67, 67, 57, 53), mu = 45)
Pythonfrom scipy.stats import ttest_1samp; ttest_1samp([69, 56, 65, 50, 72, 70, 73, 47, 59, 56, 55, 45, 39, 70, 52, 64, 67, 67, 57, 53], 45)
Red-pencil reminder. df = n − 1. For ≠, use the column α/2 for the critical value and double the table areas for the p-value.

7. The z test for a proportion p

Assumptions: a random sample; each observation is a success or failure; and np_0\ge10 and n(1-p_0)\ge10, using the null value p_0.

Z=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}\sim N(0,1)\text{ under }H_0

The p-values and rejection regions follow the same three forms as for μ.

Worked example

Defective parts

A manufacturer claims 10% of its parts are defective. An inspector samples 200 parts and finds 30 defective. Test the claim at α = 0.05, then at α = 0.01.

  1. p = proportion of all the manufacturer’s parts that are defective. H_0:p=0.10 vs H_a:p\ne0.10.
  2. Conditions: np_0=20\ge10, n(1-p_0)=180\ge10.
  3. \hat p=30/200=0.15; SE = \sqrt{0.1(0.9)/200}=0.02121; z=0.05/0.02121=2.357\approx2.36.
  4. α = 0.05: critical value 1.96, and 2.36 ≥ 1.96; p-value = 2(1 − 0.9909) = 0.0182 < 0.05. Reject H₀: the defect rate differs from 10%.
  5. α = 0.01: critical value 2.576, and 2.36 < 2.576; 0.0182 > 0.01. Fail to reject H₀. The conclusion changes with α.
Rprop.test(30, 200, p = 0.10, correct = FALSE) (its X-squared is z²)
Pythonfrom statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest; proportions_ztest(30, 200, 0.10, prop_var=0.10)
Watch out. The test’s standard error uses p_0, not \hat p: we compute everything assuming H₀ is true.
