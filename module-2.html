1. Experiments, sample spaces, and events

Probability is the limiting value of the proportion of times an outcome occurs as the number of repeated trials goes to infinity. It is the foundation of statistical inference, because inference is based on random samples.

  • Write the sample space of an experiment and describe events as sets.
  • Use unions, intersections, complements, and Venn diagrams.
  • Apply the axioms, the complement rule, and the addition rule.
  • Find probabilities with equally likely outcomes and from two-way tables.
  • Compute conditional probabilities, check independence, and use the multiplication rule.
  • Use the law of total probability and Bayes’ theorem.

An experiment is any activity or process whose outcome is subject to uncertainty. The sample space S is the collection of all possible outcomes; for a die roll, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. An event (A, B, C, …) is any subset of the sample space. A simple event has exactly one outcome (rolling a 4: {4}); a compound event has more than one (rolling an even number: {2, 4, 6}). A probability is a number between 0 and 1, inclusive.

2. Set operations and Venn diagrams

The union A\cup B (“A or B”) contains every outcome in A, in B, or in both. The intersection A\cap B (“A and B”) contains the outcomes in both. The complement A' contains every outcome in S that is not in A. The null set ∅ has no outcomes. Events A and B are mutually exclusive (disjoint) if A\cap B=\varnothing.

Worked example

A die roll

Roll one die. Let A = {2, 4, 6} (even) and B = {4, 5, 6} (more than 3).

  1. A\cup B=\{2,4,5,6\} and A\cap B=\{4,6\}.
  2. A'=\{1,3,5\} and B'=\{1,2,3\}.
  3. A and B are not mutually exclusive, because they share 4 and 6. A and A' are always mutually exclusive.

3. Axioms and properties of probability

Probability theory assigns each event A a number P(A) that measures the chance that A occurs; it can be viewed as the long-run proportion of times A occurs in many repeated trials. Toss a fair coin 100 times and the percentage of heads may be far from 50%; toss it 100,000 times and the percentage settles close to 50%.

Axioms. (1) P(A)\ge 0 for any event A. (2) P(S)=1. (3) For disjoint events A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots, P(A_1\cup A_2\cup\cdots)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(A_i).

From the axioms: P(\varnothing)=0 and 0\le P(A)\le 1 for any A.

P(A')=1-P(A) P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)

The addition rule rearranges to P(A\cap B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cup B). For three events, the inclusion–exclusion rule adds the singles, subtracts the pairs, and adds back the triple.

P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C) -P(A\cap B)-P(B\cap C)-P(A\cap C) +P(A\cap B\cap C)
Worked example

Addition rule

In a plant, 30% of parts have a surface flaw (A), 20% have a dimension flaw (B), and 8% have both.

  1. P(A\cup B)=0.30+0.20-0.08=0.42: 42% have at least one flaw.
  2. P(\text{no flaw})=1-0.42=0.58.
  3. P(A\cap B')=P(A)-P(A\cap B)=0.30-0.08=0.22: surface flaw only.
Watch out. Adding P(A) + P(B) counts the overlap twice. Subtract P(A\cap B) unless the events are mutually exclusive.

4. Equally likely outcomes

If S has N outcomes that are all equally likely, each outcome has probability 1/N, and an event A with N(A) outcomes has probability N(A)/N.

P(\text{outcome})=\frac{1}{N} P(A)=\frac{N(A)}{N}
Worked example

Two coin tosses

Toss a fair coin twice. Let A be the event that at least one toss is heads.

  1. S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}: N = 4 equally likely outcomes.
  2. A = {HH, HT, TH}, so P(A)=3/4=0.75.
  3. No heads is the complement: P(A')=P(\{TT\})=1/4=0.25=1-0.75.
Worked example

A statistics class

A class has 30 students: 15 are physically present but mentally absent and 15 are physically and mentally absent. One student is selected at random.

  1. N = 30 equally likely students; N(A) = 15 are physically present but mentally absent.
  2. P(A)=15/30=0.5.

5. Conditional probability

If B has occurred, the probability that A occurs is the conditional probability of A given B. Conditioning shrinks the sample space to B.

P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\quad P(B)>0
Worked example

Gender vs opinion

A survey asked whether the emergence of infectious diseases in other countries is a serious threat to the United States.

YesNoUndecidedTotal
Male1442288254
Female1592448231
Total30346136485
  1. A randomly chosen male answered No: P(No | Male) = 22/254 = 0.0866.
  2. A randomly chosen Yes respondent is female: P(Female | Yes) = 159/303 = 0.5248.
  3. Females: P(No | Female) = 24/231 = 0.1039, which is more than 0.0866, so females are slightly more likely than males to answer No.
  4. Undecided overall: 136/485 = 0.2804, about 28.0%.
Watch out. P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different. The event after the bar is the group you restrict to, so its total goes in the denominator.

6. The multiplication rule and independence

Rearranging the definition of conditional probability gives the multiplication rule. Events A and B are independent when knowing that B occurred does not change the probability of A.

P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)
\text{Independent} \iff P(A\mid B)=P(A) \iff P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)

It can also be shown that P(A\mid B)+P(A'\mid B)=1 and P(A)=P(A\mid B)P(B)+P(A\mid B')P(B'). Events A_1,\ldots,A_n are mutually independent if the probability of the intersection of every subset of them equals the product of their probabilities.

Worked example

Down syndrome blood test

StatusPositiveNegativeTotal
D (Down)48654
D′ (Unaffected)130739215228
Total135539275282
  1. P(Positive) = 1355/5282 = 0.2565.
  2. P(Positive | D) = 48/54 = 0.8889.
  3. 0.8889 ≠ 0.2565, so the test result and Down syndrome status are not independent: a positive result is far more likely when the condition is present.
  4. Equivalent check: P(D ∩ Positive) = 48/5282 = 0.0091, but P(D)P(Positive) = (54/5282)(0.2565) = 0.0026.
Worked example

Shoulder surgery

An operation succeeds on the first attempt with probability 0.80. If it fails, a second operation succeeds with probability 0.98.

  1. P(first fails) = 1 − 0.80 = 0.20 and P(second fails | first fails) = 1 − 0.98 = 0.02.
  2. Multiplication rule: P(both fail) = (0.20)(0.02) = 0.004.
Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive. Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent: if one happens, the other cannot.

7. Total probability and Bayes’ theorem

Let A_1,\ldots,A_k be a partition of S: mutually exclusive and exhaustive events. The law of total probability finds P(B) by adding the branch products of a tree. Bayes’ theorem reverses the condition: from the prior probabilities P(A_i) and the likelihoods P(B\mid A_i) it gives the posterior probability P(A_j\mid B).

P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(B\mid A_i)\,P(A_i)
P(A_j\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A_j)\,P(A_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(B\mid A_i)\,P(A_i)}
Worked example

A pregnancy test

55% of women who use a pregnancy test are pregnant. The test reads positive for 99% of pregnant women and negative for 99.2% of women who are not pregnant.

  1. Partition: pregnant (0.55) and not pregnant (0.45). P(+ | pregnant) = 0.99 and P(+ | not pregnant) = 1 − 0.992 = 0.008.
  2. Total probability: P(+) = 0.99(0.55) + 0.008(0.45) = 0.5445 + 0.0036 = 0.5481.
  3. Bayes: P(pregnant | +) = 0.5445/0.5481 = 0.9934.
Worked example

Three machines

Machines M1, M2, M3 make 40%, 35%, and 25% of the parts; 2%, 3%, and 5% of their parts are defective (D).

  1. P(D) = 0.02(0.40) + 0.03(0.35) + 0.05(0.25) = 0.0080 + 0.0105 + 0.0125 = 0.0310.
  2. P(M3 | D) = 0.0125/0.0310 = 0.4032. M3 makes only 25% of the parts but about 40% of the defectives.
Rprior <- c(0.40, 0.35, 0.25); pD <- c(0.02, 0.03, 0.05); joint <- prior * pD; sum(joint); joint / sum(joint)
Pythonprior = [0.40, 0.35, 0.25]; pD = [0.02, 0.03, 0.05]; joint = [a*b for a, b in zip(prior, pD)]; sum(joint), [j/sum(joint) for j in joint]
Natural frequencies. Imagine 10,000 parts: 4,000 from M1 (80 defective), 3,500 from M2 (105 defective), 2,500 from M3 (125 defective). Of the 310 defectives, 125 came from M3: 125/310 = 0.4032.

8. Summary

Describe the sample space and events with sets. Use the complement and addition rules, count equally likely outcomes, and restrict to the given group for a conditional probability. Multiply along a tree, add the branches for a total probability, and divide by that total to reverse the condition with Bayes’ theorem.
