1. Why go beyond a point estimate?

Before an election, a national poll reported that Candidate A would receive 62% of the vote. How close is 62% to the true population proportion? A point estimate alone cannot say how close it is likely to be, or how much it would vary across random samples collected the same way. Reported instead as “between 60% and 64%,” the result becomes an interval estimate: a range of values believed to contain the parameter. Its margin of error conveys the uncertainty.

  • Build and interpret z intervals for μ (σ known, and large samples with s).
  • Build the Wald interval for p (course default) and the score (Wilson) interval.
  • Find one-sided confidence bounds.
  • Use the t distribution with df = n − 1 for small samples from a normal population.
  • Build intervals for σ² and σ with the chi-squared table.
  • Explain how the confidence level and n change the width.
\text{Point estimate}\pm\text{Margin of error} \text{ME}=(\text{critical value})\times(\text{standard error})

2. An interval for μ when σ is known

Start with a random sample from a normal population whose standard deviation σ is known. From Chapter 5, \bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n), so Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1), and 95% of the time Z falls between −1.96 and 1.96.

\bar X\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} 95\%:\ \bar X\pm1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}

Here z_{\alpha/2} is the z critical value with area α/2 to its right, and 100(1 − α)% is the confidence level.

Confidence level80%90%95%99%
z_{\alpha/2}1.2821.6451.9602.576
Worked example

Highway MPG of a new sedan

MPG is N(\mu,\sigma^2=4), so σ = 2. A sample of n = 100 sedans has \bar x=28.

  1. Standard error: \sigma/\sqrt n=2/10=0.2.
  2. 95%: 28\pm1.96(0.2)=28\pm0.392, giving (27.608, 28.392).
  3. 90%: 28\pm1.645(0.2) = (27.671, 28.329). 99%: 28\pm2.576(0.2) = (27.485, 28.515).
  4. Interpretation (95%): we are 95% confident that the mean highway MPG of all these sedans is between 27.608 and 28.392.
R28 + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(0.975) * 2/sqrt(100)
Pythonfrom scipy.stats import norm; 28 + norm.ppf(0.975) * 2/100**0.5 * [-1, 1] (use NumPy for the array)

3. What “95% confident” means

If we repeatedly draw random samples of size n and build a 95% interval from each, about 95% of those intervals contain the true μ. The margin of error 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n is fixed; the center \bar X is random, so the interval itself is a random interval. Once computed, a particular interval either contains μ or it does not; “95%” describes the method.

Width. As n increases the standard error shrinks, so the interval narrows. As the confidence level increases the critical value grows, so the interval widens: the 99% MPG interval is wider than the 90% one.

Watch out. Do not say “there is a 95% probability that μ is in (27.608, 28.392).” μ is fixed; the 95% is about how often the method captures μ.

4. Large-sample intervals for μ

For a population of any shape with known σ, the CLT gives the same z interval for large n; the confidence level is then approximate. When σ is unknown, replace it by the sample standard deviation s. Course rule: use these large-sample z intervals when n > 40.

\bar x\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ (\sigma\text{ known}) \bar x\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}\ (n>40)
Worked example

Bearing diameters

A random sample of 64 bearings has mean diameter 12.4 mm and s = 1.6 mm. Find a 95% interval for μ.

  1. n = 64 > 40, so use the large-sample z interval.
  2. SE = 1.6/√64 = 0.2; ME = 1.96(0.2) = 0.392.
  3. Interval: (12.008, 12.792) mm. We are 95% confident the mean diameter of all such bearings is in this range.

5. A large-sample interval for p (Wald)

Let X be the number of successes in a random sample of size n and \hat p=X/n. When n\hat p\ge10 and n(1-\hat p)\ge10, \hat p is approximately N\big(p,\,p(1-p)/n\big). This gives the Wald interval, the course default for a proportion.

\hat p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} n\hat p\ge10,\ n(1-\hat p)\ge10
Worked example

Satisfied customers

Of 400 surveyed customers, 252 are satisfied with a new product. Build a 95% interval for the proportion of all customers who are satisfied.

  1. \hat p=252/400=0.63. Check: 252 ≥ 10 and 148 ≥ 10.
  2. SE = \sqrt{0.63(0.37)/400}=0.02414; ME = 1.96(0.02414) = 0.0473.
  3. Interval: (0.5827, 0.6773). We are 95% confident that between 58.3% and 67.7% of all customers are satisfied.
Rp <- 252/400; p + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(0.975) * sqrt(p*(1 - p)/400)
Pythonfrom statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_confint; proportion_confint(252, 400, alpha=0.05, method="normal")

6. The score (Wilson) interval for p

The Wald interval can perform poorly: its actual coverage often falls short of the stated level, it can give endpoints outside [0, 1], and it struggles for small n or \hat p near 0 or 1. The score (Wilson) interval has better coverage, always stays in [0, 1], and works well for small and moderate n. With z=z_{\alpha/2}:

\tilde p=\frac{\hat p+\frac{z^2}{2n}}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}} \tilde p\pm\frac{z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}+\frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}}

As n grows, z^2/(2n)\to0, so \tilde p\to\hat p and the score interval approaches the Wald interval.

Worked example

Extended warranty

6 of 30 randomly selected customers would pay extra for an extended warranty. Build a 95% interval for p.

  1. \hat p=0.2; n\hat p = 6 < 10, so the Wald interval is not appropriate here. Use the score interval.
  2. z^2=3.8416: \tilde p=\frac{0.2+3.8416/60}{1+3.8416/30}=\frac{0.2640}{1.1281}=0.2341.
  3. ME = \frac{1.96\sqrt{0.2(0.8)/30+3.8416/3600}}{1.1281}=0.1390.
  4. Interval: (0.0950, 0.3731).
Rprop.test(6, 30, correct = FALSE)$conf.int (the Wilson interval)
Pythonfrom statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_confint; proportion_confint(6, 30, method="wilson")

7. One-sided confidence bounds

When only one direction matters, use a confidence bound: put all of α in one tail, replacing z_{\alpha/2} with z_\alpha.

\text{Upper: }\mu<\bar x+z_\alpha\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \text{Lower: }\mu>\bar x-z_\alpha\frac{s}{\sqrt n}

Interpretation: we are 100(1 − α)% confident that μ is less than (or greater than) the bound. Bounds for p use the same replacement in the Wald interval. For 95% bounds, z_{0.05}=1.645.

Worked example

An upper bound for bearing diameter

  1. 95% upper bound: 12.4+1.645(0.2)=12.729 mm.
  2. We are 95% confident the mean diameter is less than 12.729 mm.
Watch out. A 95% bound uses 1.645, not 1.96. The two-sided 95% interval splits 5% into two tails; a bound puts the whole 5% in one.

8. Small samples: the t distribution

When n ≤ 40 a large-sample interval may not be valid. Assume a random sample from a normal population (or at least symmetric and unimodal) with unknown μ and σ. Replacing σ by s then gives a t distribution.

\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}

The t distribution with ν degrees of freedom, t_\nu, is bell-shaped, symmetric, unimodal, centered at 0, and has heavier tails than N(0, 1); as ν → ∞ it approaches N(0, 1). Its density is f(x;\nu)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac\nu2)}\big(1+\frac{x^2}{\nu}\big)^{-(\nu+1)/2}. The critical value t_{\alpha,\nu} has area α to its right. Course rule: use df = n − 1 exactly.

\bar x\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \mu<\bar x+t_{\alpha,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \mu>\bar x-t_{\alpha,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}
Worked example

Three-toed sloth speeds

Sloth speeds (meters per minute) are normal with unknown μ and σ. A random sample of n = 10 has \bar x=4.6 and s^2=2.1.

  1. n = 10 ≤ 40 and the population is normal: use t with df = 9. s=\sqrt{2.1}=1.4491, SE = 1.4491/√10 = 0.4583.
  2. 90%: t_{0.05,9}=1.833; ME = 0.840; interval (3.760, 5.440).
  3. 95%: t_{0.025,9}=2.262; ME = 1.037; interval (3.563, 5.637).
  4. 99%: t_{0.005,9}=3.250; ME = 1.489; interval (3.111, 6.089).
  5. Higher confidence means a wider interval. Each t value is larger than the matching z value, because s adds uncertainty.
R4.6 + c(-1, 1) * qt(0.975, 9) * sqrt(2.1)/sqrt(10)
Pythonfrom scipy.stats import t; t.interval(0.95, 9, loc=4.6, scale=2.1**0.5/10**0.5)
Red-pencil reminder. Use df = n − 1, not n. Use s (the square root of s²) in the standard error. And use z, not t, only when σ is known or n > 40.

9. Intervals for σ² and σ

The chi-squared distribution with ν df is not symmetric; it has mean ν and variance 2ν. If Z\sim N(0,1) then Z^2\sim\chi^2_1, and a sum of n independent squared standard normals is \chi^2_n. For a random sample from a normal population:

\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}

The critical value \chi^2_{\alpha,\nu} has area α to its right. Solving for σ² gives the interval below: the larger critical value \chi^2_{\alpha/2} goes with the lower endpoint. Take square roots of both endpoints for σ. For one-sided bounds, replace α/2 with α in the endpoint you keep.

\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}}<\sigma^2<\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}}
Worked example

Wind shear incidents

40 wind shear incidents had a mean microburst of 38 knots with s = 3.28. Assuming normality, find 95% intervals for σ² and σ.

  1. df = 39; s^2=10.7584 and (n-1)s^2=419.578.
  2. Table: \chi^2_{0.025,39}=58.120 and \chi^2_{0.975,39}=23.654.
  3. σ²: (419.578/58.120, 419.578/23.654) = (7.219, 17.738).
  4. σ: (√7.219, √17.738) = (2.687, 4.212) knots.
Rv <- 39 * 3.28^2; v / qchisq(c(0.975, 0.025), 39)
Pythonfrom scipy.stats import chi2; v = 39 * 3.28**2; v/chi2.ppf(0.975, 39), v/chi2.ppf(0.025, 39)
Watch out. The σ² interval is not symmetric about s², and the bigger table value produces the smaller endpoint. Check that your lower endpoint is below s² and your upper endpoint above it.
