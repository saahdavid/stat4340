1. Two discrete random variables

When two random variables X and Y are defined on the same sample space, their joint probability mass function gives the probability of each pair of values.

  • Find probabilities, marginal distributions, and expectations from a joint PMF or joint PDF.
  • Decide whether two random variables are independent.
  • Compute covariance and correlation and use Var(X ± Y).
  • Distinguish a parameter from a statistic and a population distribution from a sampling distribution.
  • Use E(X̄) = μ, σ_X̄ = σ/√n, the Law of Large Numbers, and the Central Limit Theorem.
p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y) p(x,y)\ge 0 \sum_x\sum_y p(x,y)=1
P\big((X,Y)\in A\big)=\sum_{(x,y)\in A}p(x,y)

A can be any set of pairs, such as \{(x,y):x>2,\,y<4\} or \{(x,y):\min(x,y)>1\}.

Worked example

A joint PMF table

X \ Y01234
01/21/161/321/321/32
11/161/321/321/321/32
21/321/321/321/321/32
  1. P(X > 1, Y > 2): only X = 2 with Y = 3 or 4: 1/32 + 1/32 = 1/16 = 0.0625.
  2. P(max(X, Y) < 1): both X and Y must be 0: p(0, 0) = 1/2.

2. Marginal PMFs

The marginal PMF of X adds the joint PMF across each row (over all y); the marginal PMF of Y adds down each column (over all x).

p_X(x)=\sum_y p(x,y) p_Y(y)=\sum_x p(x,y)
Worked example

Marginals of the table

  1. Row sums: p_X(0)=\tfrac{21}{32}=0.65625, p_X(1)=\tfrac{6}{32}=0.1875, p_X(2)=\tfrac{5}{32}=0.15625.
  2. Column sums: p_Y(0)=\tfrac{19}{32}=0.59375, p_Y(1)=\tfrac{4}{32}=0.125, and p_Y(2)=p_Y(3)=p_Y(4)=\tfrac{3}{32}=0.09375.
  3. P(X = 1) = 0.1875 and P(Y ≤ 1) = 0.59375 + 0.125 = 0.71875.

3. Two continuous random variables

For continuous X and Y the joint PDF f(x, y) gives probabilities as volumes under its surface.

f(x,y)\ge 0 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1
P\big(a\le X\le b,\ c\le Y\le d\big) =\int_a^b\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx

The marginal PDFs integrate out the other variable.

f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx
Worked example

A joint density

Let f(x,y)=\frac{x(1+3y^2)}{4} for 0 < x < 2, 0 < y < 1.

  1. Valid: f ≥ 0 and \frac14\int_0^2 x\,dx\int_0^1(1+3y^2)\,dy=\frac14(2)(2)=1.
  2. P(0<X<1,\ \tfrac14<Y<\tfrac12)=\frac14\int_0^1x\,dx\int_{1/4}^{1/2}(1+3y^2)\,dy=\frac14\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac{23}{64}=0.0449.
  3. P(X>1,Y<1)=\frac14\int_1^2 x\,dx\cdot 2=\frac14\cdot\frac32\cdot 2=0.75.
  4. Marginals: f_X(x)=\frac{x}{2} on (0, 2) and f_Y(y)=\frac{1+3y^2}{2} on (0, 1).
  5. P(X>1)=\int_1^2\frac x2\,dx=0.75; P(0<Y<\tfrac14)=\frac12\big(\tfrac14+\tfrac1{64}\big)=0.1328; and P(X=2)+P(Y=1)=0: single values have no probability.

4. Independence of two random variables

X and Y are independent when the joint distribution factors into the marginals everywhere; otherwise they are dependent.

p(x,y)=p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)\ \text{for all }(x,y) f(x,y)=f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\ \text{for all }(x,y)
Worked example

Checking independence

  1. For f(x,y)=\frac{x(1+3y^2)}{4}: f_X(x)f_Y(y)=\frac x2\cdot\frac{1+3y^2}{2}=f(x,y) on the whole rectangle, so X and Y are independent.
  2. For the joint PMF table: p(0,0)=0.5 but p_X(0)p_Y(0)=0.65625(0.59375)=0.3896. One failing pair is enough: X and Y are dependent.
Watch out. Independence must hold for every pair. One pair where the product matches proves nothing; one pair where it fails proves dependence.

5. Expected values of h(X, Y)

E[h(X,Y)]=\sum_x\sum_y h(x,y)\,p(x,y) E[h(X,Y)]=\iint h(x,y)\,f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
Worked example

Expectations from the table

  1. From the marginals: E(X) = 0.1875 + 2(0.15625) = 0.5 and E(Y) = 0.125 + 2(0.09375) + 3(0.09375) + 4(0.09375) = 0.96875.
  2. E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y) = 1.46875.
  3. E(X^2+Y^2)=0.8125+2.84375=3.65625.
  4. E(2X^3+18)=2E(X^3)+18=2(1.4375)+18=20.875.
Worked example

A density with a constant

Let f(x,y)=x+cy^2 for 0 < x < 1, 0 < y < 1.

  1. \int_0^1\!\int_0^1(x+cy^2)\,dx\,dy=\frac12+\frac c3=1, so c=\frac32.
  2. E(XY)=\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 xy\big(x+\tfrac32y^2\big)\,dx\,dy=\frac16+\frac3{16}=\frac{17}{48}=0.3542.
  3. E(X^2)=\frac14+\frac16=\frac5{12} and E(Y^2)=\frac16+\frac3{10}=\frac7{15}, so E(X^2+Y^2)=0.8833.

6. Covariance and correlation

The covariance measures how X and Y vary together; the correlation rescales it to lie between −1 and 1.

\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)] \text{Cov}(X,Y)=E(XY)-\mu_X\mu_Y
\text{Cov}(aX,bY)=ab\,\text{Cov}(X,Y) \text{Var}(X)=\text{Cov}(X,X) \text{Var}(X\pm Y)=\text{Var}(X)+\text{Var}(Y) \pm2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)
\rho_{X,Y}=\text{Corr}(X,Y)=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y} -1\le\rho\le 1
  • If Cov(X, Y) = 0, X and Y are uncorrelated.
  • Corr(aX + b, cY + d) = Corr(X, Y) when a and c have the same sign.
  • Independent ⇒ uncorrelated, but uncorrelated does not imply independent.
  • \rho=\pm1 exactly when Y = aX + b for some a ≠ 0.
Worked example

Covariance for f(x, y) = x + 1.5y²

  1. E(X)=\frac13+\frac14=\frac7{12} and E(Y)=\frac14+\frac38=\frac58.
  2. \text{Cov}(X,Y)=\frac{17}{48}-\frac7{12}\cdot\frac58=-\frac1{96}=-0.0104.
  3. \sigma_X^2=\frac5{12}-\big(\frac7{12}\big)^2=0.0764 and \sigma_Y^2=\frac7{15}-\big(\frac58\big)^2=0.0760.
  4. \rho=\frac{-0.0104}{\sqrt{0.0764(0.0760)}}=-0.137: a weak negative linear relationship.
Worked example

Two coin flips

X and Y count heads on the first and second of two independent fair flips.

  1. Each of the four pairs (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1) has p(x, y) = 1/4.
  2. E(X) = E(Y) = 1/2 and E(XY) = P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 1/4.
  3. Cov(X, Y) = 1/4 − (1/2)(1/2) = 0, so ρ = 0, as independence requires.
Red-pencil reminder. Var(X − Y) adds the variances: Var(X) + Var(Y) − 2Cov(X, Y). Variances never subtract.

7. Parameters, statistics, and random samples

A parameter is a numerical characteristic of the population: the mean income of all airplane pilots in the US, or the percentage of all Texas college students with triple-digit student debt. It is fixed but unknown. A statistic is computed from a random sample, such as the mean income of a random sample of 50 pilots. It is our best guess for the parameter, and because it varies from sample to sample it is a random variable.

A random sample X_1,\ldots,X_n is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). The difference between a statistic and its parameter is random sampling error. The population distribution describes the variable across all individuals; the sampling distribution of a statistic describes its values across all possible samples of the same size.

Example question

Speed of airplanes

A study estimates the average cruising speed of commercial airplanes from a random sample of n = 10 planes. How much would the sample mean vary from sample to sample, and what happens to that variability as n grows? The next two sections answer both questions.

8. The sample mean and the Law of Large Numbers

For a random sample from a population with mean μ and variance σ², the sample mean is centered at μ and its spread shrinks with √n.

E(\bar X)=\mu_{\bar X}=\mu \text{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n} \sigma_{\bar X}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}

Law of Large Numbers. As n increases, \bar X gets closer to μ: \bar X\to\mu as n → ∞. The uncertainty in the sample mean decreases as n increases.

Worked example

Polar bears

Weights of Arctic polar bears have mean 450 kg and variance 9\text{ kg}^2. A random sample of n = 10 bears is taken.

  1. E(\bar X)=450 kg.
  2. \sigma=\sqrt9=3 kg, so \sigma_{\bar X}=3/\sqrt{10}=0.9487 kg.
Watch out. Use σ (not σ²) in σ/√n. And σ/√n describes the spread of sample means, not of individual values.

9. The Central Limit Theorem

Let X_1,\ldots,X_n be a random sample from a population with finite mean μ and finite variance σ². Whatever the shape of the population, the sampling distribution of \bar X approaches a normal distribution as n increases.

\bar X\approx N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\ \text{for large }n z=\frac{\bar x-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
  • Any population (finite μ and σ²): \bar X is approximately normal when n is large. Course rule: use the CLT when n > 40.
  • Normal population: \bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n) exactly, for every n.
Worked example

A CLT probability

Suppose polar bear weights have mean 450 kg and standard deviation 21 kg (shape unknown), and n = 49 bears are sampled. Find P(\bar X>455).

  1. n = 49 > 40, so the CLT applies: \bar X\approx N(450,\ 21^2/49).
  2. \sigma_{\bar X}=21/\sqrt{49}=3 kg.
  3. z=(455-450)/3=1.6667\approx 1.67; P(\bar X>455)=1-0.9525=0.0475.
R1 - pnorm(455, 450, 21/sqrt(49))

Simulating it. Toss a fair coin 50 times and record the proportion of heads, Y_i=X_i/50 with X_i\sim\text{Bin}(50,0.5). Repeat the experiment 10, 100, 500, then 1000 times: the histogram of the Y_i fills in a bell shape centered at 0.5.

10. Summary

Joint distributions give probabilities for pairs; marginals add (or integrate) out the other variable; independence means the joint factors into the marginals. Covariance and correlation measure linear association. A statistic is a random variable with a sampling distribution: \bar X is centered at μ with SD σ/√n, and for n > 40 it is approximately normal.

p_X(x)=\sum_y p(x,y) \text{Cov}=E(XY)-\mu_X\mu_Y \rho=\tfrac{\text{Cov}}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y} \bar X\approx N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)
